Open Policy Analysis for AI and the economy

Step 1

One policy, two sides.

California's Proposition 40 proposed a one-time tax on the wealth of billionaires. It was put to voters on 3 November 2026.

One person supports it. Another opposes it. Why?

Step 2

With common facts, we learn what people value.

Suppose both start from the same facts: one analysis that anyone can inspect.

They can still disagree. One weighs the revenue more. The other weighs the people who would leave the state more. Their choices show what each of them values.

The colours stand for their values.

Step 3

With dueling certitudes, we learn nothing.

In practice each side brings its own analysis. One found about $100 billion in revenue. The other found about −$39 billion for the state. A voter cannot see where the difference comes from.

Each person takes the analysis that fits. We see which facts they chose. We learn nothing about what they value.

Step 4

One study, closed.

A published policy analysis is often a number and a description of the method.

There is nothing an outsider can run.

Step 5

One study, opened.

This is an Open Policy Analysis (OPA). The same study, in layers.

  • Materials. Code and data anyone can run.
  • Details. Documentation that traces every step.
  • Assumptions and output. An interactive page where a reader changes inputs and sees the result.

By hand, it took several months of work per analysis (wealth tax in 2019, deworming in 2021). In 2026, with agentic coding, one was built for a study of AI and the labor market. Then three were built in about a week: the two rival analyses of California's Proposition 40 and their comparison.

Step 6

Two studies, compared.

California's Proposition 40, a tax on the wealth of billionaires. Boll, Saez and Zucman found about $100 billion in revenue. Jaros and Rauh found about −$39 billion for the state.

With an OPA of each, a third page compares them. Each lever moves one disputed input from one analysis's value to the other's. The reader sees which inputs make the gap.

Below: the comparison explorer built for Proposition 40 in 2026.

Step 7

From two reports to a few inputs.

With an OPA of each analysis and a comparison, both sides can start from one set of facts.

Even when they do not, the debate narrows: from two reports that cannot be read side by side to the few inputs that drive the difference.

For Proposition 40, three inputs explain most of the gap.

Step 8

Now, AI and the economy.

One analysis: "Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI" (Korinek and others, 2026). It projects US GDP, the labor share, wages and unemployment to 2030 under three AI scenarios.

To most readers it is a set of numbers: a black box.

Step 9

The analysis as published.

The paper gives its equations and its parameters in detail. No code or data were published.

The output and the details are there. The materials, and a way for a reader to change the assumptions, are not. On the census scale it scores 3 out of 7.

Step 10

The same analysis, as an OPA.

  • Materials. The model, rebuilt from the paper, with its code public and 129 tests.
  • Details. A written reproduction that walks every table and equation in the paper's order.
  • Assumptions and output. An explorer over the paper's seven scenario dials, 4,374 combinations.

Step 11

The next debate is not known yet.

We do not know which debate about AI and the economy will come next, or which analyses it will rest on.

What can be done now is to track all of them and be ready: a census.

Step 12

Zoom out to a whole field.

The debate about the effects of AI on the economy is likely to produce many disputes like this one.

The census lists 554 quantitative analyses of AI and the US economy. Each cube is one of them.
